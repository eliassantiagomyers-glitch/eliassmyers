Restaurants are too expensive and everything sucks

2026-10-06

The question: why does everything suck? has been stuck in my head. As I listened to a recent episode of The Daily, in which the host explained how the entire restaurant business was broken, that question reemerged; it seemed as though nothing can be afforded, that the business model of providing services, food service or other, to working and middle classes --- the 99% as some would have it --- is impossible not because current economic constraints (gas prices due to the war in Iran, for one), but because of the natural growth/change in the American economy. Borrowing a dead horse: the unaffordability is the point. We can't afford anything any more and can't do anything any more because of the natural laws of American capitalism.

The inability to offer restaurant services at price points available to all is a scary marker in our descent into a K shaped economy. Only restaurants that cater to the upper and upper middle classes are holding on, he noted. I think this is just the beginning; soon, we'll slip further into unaffordability for the well-off, and on and on, until economic power constrains everyone and is leveraged for control. That is to say: the Italian restaurant that is holding on by shaving truffles over pasta for $15 a shave is not protected forever. As this K economy passes what's left of the upper middle class, the cohort of consumers who can afford small luxuries will shrink. The economic gap will grow and those hanging on will shrink.

Every so many months, I read articles about what is and isn't growing in the American economy. Daily commodities, things that everyone needs -- groceries, for one -- are in withdrawal: they can't be afforded anymore. Luxuries, however, continue to grow. This isn't because average Americans are buying more luxuries, it's because the portion of the economy that's growing is at the top, meanwhile the average American is in economic withdrawal.

I've thought a lot about why we see the mid 20th century as a "golden era," and how an economic period of that kind was possible. The answer I've come to, I think, is natural to societies in capital systems: there's always economic winners and economic losers -- in fact, the existence of one allows the existence of the other, this is critical and well established. Turning to today, it feels evident who the economic winners and losers are. But this same equilibrium existed during the 50s, 60s, and 70s as well. The losers at that point were just everyone one else in the world: Europe was decimated, Asia was decimated, South and Central America were decimated, and on and on and on. American success in this period was also in a K shape, but instead of today's local/domestic K, it was a global K --- the American middle class was actually a global 1%, propped up by the strength of the US dollar, which was backed by the strength of the US military. I guess this goes to say I don't think there's ever been an equitable era of democratic wealth. If the economic losers aren't in the US, they're abroad. We're just seeing the growth of the domestic K as the global economy has grown to compete with the US.

Returning to my original question, why does everything suck? I think the answer lies in perspective. It sucks for Americans today not because it's getting worse, but because we're experiencing the realities that have always existed in capital systems. Can I say "the chickens have come home to roost"? I don't know. Will/can it improve? No, because this isn't a problem within the system, it's a structural reality of the system as a whole.

I was also struck by the guest's remenicanse of urban life. He goes on about how moving to NYC wasn't a lifestyle choice, as much as it was an affordability choice. Will that day ever return? What would that take? Another white flight, Robert Moses-view of urban living? I don't think cities can ever be affordable in the US while they remain popular amongst wealthy Americans. That's not an economic paradox, rather it's a geographic one. Simply put, America does not have enough city space. Besides NYC, SF/Oakland, Chicago, Seattle, Boston, Philly and DC, (maybe Portland), I'd argue there's no other urban space --- and I mean truly urban --- for such a massive country. A lot of "cities" in the US are actually collectives of suburbs -- for instance LA, Miami, Austin, Houston --- and offer nothing of the desired urban lifestyle (a litmus test for this could be: do you need a car? and for the most urban spaces: can you even have a car?) With such limited urban space, we're left to simple supply and demand. The city, while sought after by the wealthy, will never be affordable to the average American, as there will never be enough city to fill the demand. Perhaps that might change by massive increases in housing stock, but given our current pace, I doubt it.
