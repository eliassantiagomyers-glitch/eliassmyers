The 9/11 Memorial is a great place for influencers

2026-10-23

The 9/11 memorial and museum are split into two areas. The memorial, a park with two enormous black holes sinking unknowingly deep into the ground, sits street level. The memorial was less of a memorial and more of a mixed use space, hosting a swath of vendors selling USA tee shirts, scantily-clad Eastern-European Instagram models leaning over the cavity that was the North Tower hoping to capture what appeared to be upskirt thirst trap teases, and a couple of Chasids asking “Jewish? Jewish?” to any white man, tying tefillin around the arms of their lucky finds.

The museum was less of a museum and more a gallery of gore porn, a frozen frame of the raw, unnatural decomposition of goodness and humanity that died that day. I was caught. My attention was broken just three times, to send Callum and Fiona “This is really rough” followed twenty minutes later with “This is insane,” and one long hour later, “Wow. This is fucking terrible.” I left in a state of bewilderment and fury, untrusting my own response.

A chunk of American Airlines flight 77 was on display – a warped, paint stripped window from the left side of the fuselage. I reached out and touched it. An anger bubbled within me. This is real. I’m touching the plane. I experienced — for the first time ever — the patriotic white knuckled anger of war hawks and Toby Keith and all those who post #neverforget every year.

I, who’ve spent the greater part of my adult life (for lack of a better word) worshiping this dark day, understanding it as a turning point in American history (the post 9/11 era, I’ve called it), was caught flat footed. In that moment, there was no context or implication or holistic reading of 9/11 or the invasion of Iraq or the Global War on Terror. Down, three stories below Ground Zero, there was just anger.
