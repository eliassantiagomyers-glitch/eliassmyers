Hills Like White Elephants

2025-01-25

The Hills as seen from Girona, Spain

I’m sitting on a train to Barcelona. The train—de alta velocidad— has an advertised top speed of 187 MPH, but the speedometer on my phone reads 152. It’ll probably speed up—we aren’t far from the station.  

I spent the weekend in Madrid visiting friends from Berkeley. I walked thousands of steps but still feel like I didn’t see much. Most of those steps were museum steps, and most of those museum steps were in the Reina Sofia. I saw Guernica twice, once last night and once this morning. I wasn’t done looking when they closed the museum. 

It feels weird to think about how long I’ve been here. I landed on my 21st birthday, January 9th. It wasn’t until I left the city for the first time that I recognized the familiarity I’ve developed with it. Even in Madrid I caught myself eager to return home to the little backyard hut I’m renting in Gracia, with the pigeons on the roof and the little boy who screams with laughter at his YouTube shorts. 

I’ve done a lot over the last two and a half weeks. Last weekend, I took the train up to Girona, a small old town along the French border. It was 45 minutes outside of Barcelona, and the views were stunning. In the distance, you could see snow covered peaks. I’m pretty sure they were the Pyrenees. I thought of a story I read once years ago—Hills Like White Elephants by Ernest Hemingway. I joked that the mountains were like the hills in the story, but when I re-read the story on the train ride home, I realized that they probably were. It takes place at a train station in northeastern Spain, about 45 minutes outside of Barcelona. Maybe Hemingway was inspired by the same views I was—maybe he was even standing in the same spot that I was! From that, he wrote a masterpiece, and I, this blog post. Oh well. 

I’m spending the next two weekends in France. I’ll be in Toulouse with Georgia and a cohort of people she’s met in Bordeaux. I’m excited to meet more people. I knew coming in to this the type of person who studies abroad in Spain, but I’ve done a decent job meeting other people. The weekend after that, I’m going cave and ruin exploring with Val, up in those same mountains between France and Spain. 


The speedometer on my phone reads 176 MPH. Maybe this train will max out. 

I’m trying to find my place in Barcelona—it’s a city protective of its own, and cold to the outside. I understand why—in ways I’ve never seen in San Francisco, Barcelona is drowning in tourists. But it’s created a binary (or at least I have): tourist or local. Outside my building is a large, spray-painted sentence that reads “Tourists Go Home.” It’s on the next street over too, and on buildings across the city. Every day on my walk to the metro, I think about it. Where am I in this binary? By being here, am I only contributing to the “outsider pollution”? Luckily enough, my Spanish is proficient. Luckily enough, I can play both sides of the dichotomy long enough to start a conversation. Or at least long enough for them to ask where in Asia I’m from. It’s a comforting fact that that’s universal. 

The train’s starting to slow down. I think we’re pulling into our first stop.
